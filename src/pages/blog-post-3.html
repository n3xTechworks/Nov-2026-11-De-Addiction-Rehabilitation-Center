What you are feeling is normal

Worry, anger, guilt, embarrassment and exhaustion often all arrive at once when someone we love is struggling with substance use. Many families spend years trying to manage the problem quietly. If that sounds familiar, please know that you did not cause this, you cannot control it alone, and help is available for you as well as for them.

How to start the conversation

Choose a calm moment when your loved one is sober and you are not rushed. Talk about specific things you have noticed and how they affected you, rather than labels. For example: “I noticed you missed work twice last week and I felt scared” is easier to hear than “You are an alcoholic.”

  • Use “I” statements and keep your voice gentle.
  • Listen more than you speak, and ask open questions.
  • Have information ready, such as a confidential helpline number, but do not push.
  • Expect more than one conversation. Change often takes time.

An approach that helps: CRAFT

Community Reinforcement and Family Training (CRAFT) is an evidence-based approach that teaches families to reward sober behaviour, let natural consequences happen and spot moments when a person may be open to help. Studies show it helps around two in three families get a reluctant loved one into treatment, more than confrontational “interventions”.

Kind, clear boundaries

A boundary is a decision about what you will do, not an attempt to control someone else. It might be: “I will not give you money, but I will happily buy groceries,” or “If you drive after drinking, I will take the keys.” Say it once, calmly, and follow through. Boundaries protect your wellbeing and often help a loved one see the impact of their use.

Love and limits can live together. In fact, recovery usually needs both.

Looking after yourself

Living alongside addiction is stressful, and family members often stop sleeping, eating well or seeing friends. Keep your own routines, talk to someone you trust and consider a support group such as Al-Anon or our weekly family circle. If there are children at home, explain things in simple words and reassure them it is not their fault.

If anyone is at risk of harm, call emergency services on 112 first.

Key points

  • Calm, specific conversations work better than confrontation.
  • Boundaries protect you and are not a punishment.
  • Your own support matters: you cannot pour from an empty cup.
