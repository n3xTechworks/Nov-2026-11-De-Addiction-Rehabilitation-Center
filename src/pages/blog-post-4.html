Cravings are like waves

A craving can feel as if it will keep growing until you give in. In reality, cravings behave more like waves: they build, reach a peak and then fade, usually within 20 to 30 minutes. Each time you let a wave pass without acting on it, the next one tends to be a little smaller. This idea, called urge surfing, is a core part of mindfulness-based relapse prevention (MBRP).

Why mindfulness helps

Mindfulness simply means paying attention to the present moment, on purpose and without judgement. Studies show that mindfulness-based programmes reduce cravings and lower relapse rates, partly by creating a small pause between the trigger and the response. In that pause, you have a choice.

A five-minute urge-surfing practice

  1. Pause and name it. Say to yourself, “This is a craving.” Naming it puts a little space between you and the urge.
  2. Find it in your body. Is it tightness in the chest, restlessness in the hands, a dry mouth? Describe it with curiosity.
  3. Breathe with it. Breathe slowly in for four counts and out for six. Imagine your breath as a surfboard riding the wave.
  4. Watch it change. Notice how the sensations shift, grow or soften. Nothing has to be fixed.
  5. Choose your next step. When the wave eases, do something kind: drink water, text a friend, step outside.

You do not have to fight the craving. You only have to stay with it until it passes.

Making it a habit

Like any skill, mindfulness gets easier with practice. Try five minutes each morning, attached to something you already do, such as your first cup of tea. Free apps and short guided recordings can help. At Harborlight, residents practise together each morning in the garden pavilion, and outpatients can join online.

When to ask for more help

Mindfulness is a tool, not a cure. If cravings feel overwhelming, are getting stronger or come with thoughts of self-harm, please talk to your doctor or counsellor straight away. Medication and other therapies can help a great deal alongside these practices.

Key points

  • Cravings rise, peak and fall, usually within 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Noticing a craving without acting on it weakens it over time.
  • Short daily practice is more helpful than occasional long sessions.
