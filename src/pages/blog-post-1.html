What doctors mean by addiction

Doctors use the term substance use disorder to describe a pattern of alcohol or drug use that keeps going despite clear harm to health, work, money or relationships. It is diagnosed in the same way as other health conditions: by looking at specific signs over time, such as strong cravings, needing more to feel the same effect, withdrawal when stopping, and repeated attempts to cut down that have not worked.

The diagnosis runs on a scale from mild to severe. Many people sit at the milder end and never think of themselves as having a problem, while others have been struggling for years. Wherever someone is, help is available, and earlier support tends to be easier.

What changes in the brain

Alcohol, opioids, cannabis, nicotine and stimulants all act on the brain's reward system, which normally helps us repeat things that keep us alive, such as eating and connecting with others. With repeated use, the brain adapts. Everyday pleasures feel flatter, the substance feels more necessary, and cues such as a place, a time of day or a feeling can trigger powerful cravings.

At the same time, the parts of the brain that manage stress and help us pause before acting work less well. This is why a person can sincerely want to stop and still find it very hard to do so on willpower alone. It is not weakness. It is the result of real changes, and most of them ease with time and treatment.

Why some people are more affected than others

No single cause explains addiction. Genes account for roughly half of a person's risk. The rest comes from life: early exposure, trauma, ongoing stress, loneliness, untreated anxiety or depression, chronic pain and the people around us. Understanding these factors is not about blame. It helps us plan care that treats the whole person and not only the substance.

Why language and shame matter

Words such as “addict” or “junkie” reduce a person to one part of their life, and research shows they make doctors, employers and families less likely to offer help. We say a person with a substance use disorder or a person in recovery. It may seem small, but people who feel judged often delay treatment for years.

Nobody chooses to develop an addiction. Everybody deserves care that treats them with dignity.

Treatment works

Like diabetes or high blood pressure, substance use disorders respond to a mix of medical care, therapy and lifestyle change. Evidence-based options include:

  • Medically supervised detox to manage withdrawal safely and comfortably.
  • Talking therapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT).
  • Medication-assisted treatment for alcohol and opioid use, which lowers cravings and relapse risk.
  • Family therapy and peer support, including optional 12-step groups.

Relapse rates are similar to other long-term conditions. A return to use is a sign that the plan needs adjusting, not that treatment has failed or that the person cannot recover.

Taking a first step

If you are worried about yourself or someone close to you, a confidential conversation is a good place to start. You do not need to be certain, and you do not need to have hit rock bottom. You only need to be curious about whether things could be different.

This article is general information and does not replace an assessment by a qualified clinician.

Key points

  • Addiction is a recognised, treatable health condition, not a failure of character.
  • Repeated substance use changes reward, stress and self-control systems in the brain.
  • Recovery is common, and outcomes are similar to other long-term conditions.
